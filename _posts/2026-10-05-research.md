As a PhD student, I've realized that conducting good research is a skill that must be continuously learned and refined. One book that has shaped my understanding of rigorous research is *Craft of Research* by Wayne C. Booth, Gregory G. Colomb, and Joseph M. Williams. Here are the key lessons I've internalized so far.

## Overview

The foundation of good research lies in understanding that research is not just about gathering data or finding answers—it's about making a contribution to knowledge in a systematic and credible way. This requires developing habits of mind around curiosity, rigor, and clear communication.

- Research is a conversation with your field
- It requires both independent thinking and respect for existing work
- Methods improve with deliberate practice and feedback
- The researcher's process is as important as the final product

## Research Question

A strong research question is the anchor of everything that follows. It should be specific enough to guide your work but broad enough to yield meaningful insights. A good question is one that you're genuinely curious about and that your field hasn't fully answered yet.

- Start with a problem you notice, not just a topic
- Refine the question iteratively as you learn more
- Ask "so what?" repeatedly until you reach genuine significance
- Make sure your question is researchable with available methods and resources
- A well-framed question saves countless hours later

## Making Arguments

Making arguments is about building a logical chain of reasoning that supports your claims. This isn't about being persuasive for persuasion's sake—it's about constructing evidence-based arguments that are intellectually honest and sound.

- Ground arguments in evidence, not assumptions
- Anticipate counterarguments and address them directly
- Use a logical structure: claim → evidence → reasoning → significance
- Avoid logical fallacies and circular reasoning
- Build arguments incrementally; don't expect readers to make large conceptual leaps

## Delivering Arguments

Even the strongest arguments fall flat if they're poorly communicated. Delivering arguments effectively means organizing your thoughts clearly, using precise language, and ensuring your reader can follow your logic without strain.

- Structure your writing with your reader in mind
- Use topic sentences to signal what each section contributes
- Revise ruthlessly for clarity—ambiguity wastes your reader's time
- Show the connection between your evidence and your claim explicitly
- Different contexts require different formats (papers, presentations, posters)

## Tips from My PI

I'm incredibly lucky to have an advisor who takes considerable interest in his team's work and yet allows us freedom and flexibility to follow our own approaches and directions of interest.
My advisor has been instrumental in shaping how I approach research. The wisdom gained from working closely with an experienced researcher is invaluable and often surpasses what you learn from any book!

- Communication is very important. Ask good questions. Communicate your ideas clearly.
- Test yourself whenever possible
- Keep learning about the latest technology - inside and outside your field. Don't be afraid to try new things and get your hands dirty.
- Update your notes.
- When it comes to scientific communication - show, don't tell.

## Miscellaneous

Beyond the core pillars of research, there are countless small practices and insights that accumulate to form good research habits. These tend to be contextual and personal, but no less important.

- Read widely, not just in your narrow area
- Keep a research journal for reflections and ideas
- Don't underestimate the importance of taking breaks
- Your research identity evolves—be patient with yourself
- Seek out writing groups and accountability partners
- Remember that research is a marathon, not a sprint
